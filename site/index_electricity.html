Abstract

Access to electricity remains one of the most consequential and unresolved development challenges in 21st century especially in the era of AI.

This report analyzes the electricity access across five world regions and nine major countries between 1990s and 2022. This analysis uses the household-level access data from the World Bank World Development Indicators (indicator EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS) compiled by Our World in Data. Based on the data and our analysis, we have some interesting findings that the development or the electricity is pretty not equally developed: East Asia and Pacific achieved near-universal access by 2020 largely through China's national electricity development campaigns. South Asia compressed a generation of infrastructure development into a single decade through India's SAUBHAGYA scheme and Bangladesh's rural cooperative model. However, Sub-Saharan Africa which includes lots of people still have no reach to electricity. The data shows that they gained only 23 percentage points more between 1996 and 2022, which shows the significant stagnation in development in the area.

Key finding: Even at the level of accelerated post-2015 pace of progress in Sub-Saharan Africa, the region will reach only about 62% access by 2030 — well short of the UN Sustainable Development Goal in electricity reachness at that time.

Section 7 — Key Findings

Key Findings and Policy Implications

+53 pp
South Asia progress, 1993–2022

South Asia has the fastest development speed in regional electrification in the dataset. This is mainly driven by the India and Bangladesh. The development makes the access rate jumps from 46.6% in 1993 to 99.1% in 2022, which demonstrates the rapid population-scale electrification is achieved with sustained political commitment and ecnomic growth.

100%
China's electrification rate, 2014

China achieved 100% of household electrification by 2014, increased from 96.7% in 2000. This can be caused by the Rural Power Grid construction and upgrading programs, which connected hundreds of millions of rural residents from the 2000 to 2014. This indicates the largest single country electrification achievement in history.

+23 pp
Sub-Saharan Africa progress, 1996–2022

In the analysis, we can see a outlier among the countries and regions. The Sub-Saharan Africa gained 23% of access rate between 1996 to 2022 with the number from 28.5% to 51.6%. There are some notable move between 1996 and 2000 as broder country coverage and population growth, but the region still accounts for roughly 85% of the global electricity gap.

2030
SDG 7 deadline — current trajectory falls short

The Sub-Saharan Africa will reach only 62% of access rate by 2030 even if they can have the accelerated post 2015 rate of about 1.5% per year, which is far behind the UN SDG 7 universal access target. This will require roughly doubling the current pace from 1.5-3.0%, which indicates the financial challenges on the order of 30-40 billion per year above current investment level.

From the findings above, we can conclude some key insights from them. The evidence from the South Asia and East Africa shows that the rapid electrification is achievable at scale with the help of goverments. If the governments can makeit to political priority with proper financial mechanisms. Moreover, the gap in Sub-Saharan Africa is not caused by technology problem because the off-grid solar is now cost competitive but the financial and government problem, which requires new models for rist sharing between public and private sectors. Then, the divergence between countries at similar income leveles like Bangladesh and Nigeria implicate that the electrification outcomes is not only depend on the GDP or the ecnomic growth but should depend on some more complex reasons like the institutional quality and policy design.

Methodology & Data Sources

Methodology

All of the figures and graphs showed in this website are constructed from the World Bank's definition that the percentage of a country's population with access to electricity. It is reported through the household surveys and utility converage estimates because there's no way to collect the 100% precise data from different countries. The primary data source is the World Bank World Development Indicators, indicator EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS, accessed via Our World in Data's curated and processed dataset.

Regional aggregates (Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, East Asia and Pacific, Latin America and Caribbean) are population-weighted averages compiled by the World Bank. GDP per capita figures used in Section 5 are approximate 2022 values in constant 2015 USD from World Bank national accounts data and should be treated as indicative rather than precise.

Baseline year selection

Although this project is framed around the period 1990–2022, the World Bank does not publish some of the 1990 observations for most regional aggregates or for several of the focus countries. Rather than back-extrapolating, we use each entity's earliest real WB observation within the 1990–2000 window as its historical baseline. The specific baseline year is shown in the bar chart tooltip in Section 3 and quoted in narrative findings. For example, South Asia, 46.6% in 1993, World aggregate the earliest WB value is 78.22% in 2000 and for Sub-Saharan Africa it is 28.5% in 1996 etc.

Interpolation

The preprocessing pipeline supports linear interpolation for interior gaps between two adjacent real WB observations, but in the current dataset all 13 displayed years (1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022) contain only real WB observations — interpolation filled_count is zero. No leading or trailing extrapolation is ever performed. In our code, the missing values outside the observed range remain null and are skipped at render time. The users can go to see scripts/preprocess.py and meta.imputation in the JSON for details.

Sub-Saharan Africa pace estimates

The "post-2015 pace of 1.5% per year" cited in the abstract and findings is computed as the slope of SSA access between 2015 and 2022, divided by 7 years. The earlier "1996–2015 pace of 0.7% per year" is computed over a 19-year window. Projections to 2030 assume this post-2015 pace continues unchanged.
